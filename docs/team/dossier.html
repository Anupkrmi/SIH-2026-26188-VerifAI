What this project actually is

In one sentence: we built a web app that a border officer opens on a screen, uploads or scans an ID document into, and within seconds it tells them — with reasons, not just a red/green light — whether anything about the document counts against it, whether it shows signs of tampering, or whether it needs a human to double-check. (Note the careful wording: we report problems found, never "this document is genuine" — see section 3 for why that distinction wins arguments.)

Why it's needed: right now, an officer checks a passport by eye, in a few seconds, under pressure, with a queue behind them. Forged photos, changed birth dates, fake stamps and expired documents can slip through. Our app does the same check, faster and more consistently, and explains exactly why it flagged something — so the officer isn't just trusting a black box.

What makes ours different from a typical "AI checks your ID" app: most such tools just run one AI model and print a confidence percentage. Ours never lets AI have the final word — see the next section.

2

The big idea, in one analogy

Think of airport security

You don't get through security because one friendly guard waves you on. You pass a passport scan, then a boarding-pass check, then sometimes a manual search — several independent checks, each one able to stop you, and none of them alone able to fully clear you. Our system checks a document the same way.

We call our version of this the Trust Ladder — three layers of checking, run in a fixed order, where each layer has a different amount of "power":

  • Cryptography (unbreakable math, like a digital wax seal) — this layer can both fully clear a document and fully reject one.
  • Rule checks (does the math on the document add up — check digits, matching fields) — this layer can reject a document, but can never by itself say "definitely genuine."
  • AI / pattern-matching (forgery detection, face matching) — this layer can only ever say "something looks off, a human should look" — it can never be the reason a document is rejected outright.
The one sentence to memorise: "Our AI can raise a hand and ask a human to look closer. It can never, by itself, get a document rejected." That single rule is what makes the whole system safe to trust, and it's true because we wrote the code so it's impossible for it to work any other way — not just a promise.

Three different questions people keep mixing up

If you learn one thing from this document, learn this. Almost every confusing question a judge asks is really asking about one of these three, and they have three different answers.

The questionPlain meaningCan we answer it?
Document consistency Does this document make sense on its own terms? Do the printed details match the machine-readable strip? Do the check-digits add up? Are the dates possible? YES
Document integrity Has this document been changed since it was signed? This is the digital-seal question. YES — where a signature exists
Document authenticity Was this document genuinely issued by the government in the first place? NO — needs an issuer registry we cannot access
Why this matters more than anything else in Q&A: when a judge asks "so can it tell me if this passport is real?", they are asking the third question. The honest answer is: "We can tell you the document is internally consistent and has not been tampered with. We cannot tell you it was genuinely issued — no system can, without a connection to the issuing authority's own records, which no one outside government has." Say that clearly and you sound like you understand the problem. Claim otherwise and one follow-up question exposes it.
This is also why our app says "No adverse signals", never "genuine". On a real uploaded document our score of 0 means "nothing we measured counted against it" — it is deliberately not a claim that the document is authentic. The screen shows both halves side by side: what we established, and what we could not.

The four things the problem statement asked for

SIH's problem statement (PS 26188) names four required parts. Here's what each one means in plain English, and what we actually built for it.

Ask 1
OCR Extraction
Plain English: read the text off the document automatically, the way your phone reads a business card. What we built: reads name, passport number, nationality, date of birth, and expiry — and for any random document you throw at it (marksheet, ID card), it figures out what it's looking at and pulls out what it can.
Ask 2
Document Validation
Plain English: check the document follows the rules a real one should — right format, dates that make sense, numbers that check out mathematically. What we built: the same check-digit maths real passports use, plus checks that the printed text matches the machine-readable strip at the bottom.
Ask 3
Tampering Detection
Plain English: spot if a photo's been swapped, text edited, or a stamp faked. What we built: two ways — a cryptographic seal that instantly proves if a single pixel changed since we first saw the document, plus four independent visual checks for the cases the seal can't cover.
Ask 4
Face Verification
Plain English: does the photo on the document match the person standing there? What we built: detect the face, check the photo is actually good enough to judge fairly, then compare it to a live camera capture — real, tested, working end to end.
One thing extra, not asked for: a tamper-proof logbook of every check we've ever run — so if there's ever an investigation later, there's a trail that can't be quietly edited. That's our answer to the problem statement's "digital trail for investigations" line.

How a final decision actually gets made

The four checks that run, and — this is the important part — the order of authority between them.

1
The digital seal (cryptography)
Can fully clear OR fully reject
We sign the document's photo and text with a digital signature the moment we first see it. If anything changes afterward — even one pixel — the signature breaks and we know instantly. No AI is involved in this check at all.
2
The rule checks
Can only reject, never clear
Do the numbers add up? Does the printed date match the machine-readable code? If a rule is broken, that's an instant reject — no partial credit for "the photo looked fine."
3
Forgery-pattern AI
Can only raise a flag
Looks for pasted photos, edited regions, and screen-recaptured images. Can push a document up to "needs a second look" — never straight to "reject."
4
Face match
Can only raise a flag
Compares the document photo to a live camera capture. If the camera photo is too blurry or dark to judge fairly, it says so honestly instead of guessing.

Questions almost anyone might ask easy

Simple, common-sense questions — you should be able to answer every one of these without thinking hard.

What is OCR?
Short for "Optical Character Recognition" — software that reads printed or handwritten text out of an image, the same tech that lets your phone scan a document into text.
What does "risk score" mean here?
A number from 0 to 100 that sums up how suspicious a document looks, sorted into four bands — Low, Medium, High, Critical — each with a suggested action, like "no action needed" or "send for a closer look."
Why use AI instead of just checking manually?
Speed and consistency. A human check takes a few minutes and gets tired after the hundredth passport of the day. Ours takes seconds and applies the exact same standard every single time — and it never lets AI overrule the parts that are provably certain.
Is this using ChatGPT or a big AI model like that?
No. We use small, specialised, purpose-built models for specific jobs — reading text, detecting a face, spotting a pasted photo. A general chatbot-style model isn't designed for this kind of precise, provable work, and we didn't want a black box we can't fully explain.
What happens if the system makes a mistake?
The worst a mistake can do is send a genuine document for an unnecessary human review, or (much rarer) miss something a human then has to catch. It can never, by design, get an innocent document flat-out rejected by AI alone, and it can never silently wave through a document that failed a rule or a digital-signature check.
Can this replace the border officer?
No, and it's not meant to. It's a tool that does the tedious, error-prone first pass and hands the officer a clear, evidenced summary — the officer still makes the actual call, especially anything flagged as suspicious.
Does it work on Aadhaar cards / driving licences / not just passports?
Yes — we built a second mode specifically for that: upload any identity document, and the system figures out what type it is and runs whatever checks make sense for it. It's honest when a check doesn't apply — e.g. a marksheet has no photo, so it won't pretend to compare a face.
How is this "cybersecurity", not just AI?
Real digital-signature cryptography (the same category of maths banks and passports use) plus a tamper-evident logbook of every decision. That's the security half of the brief, alongside the AI half.
Where does it run — does it need the internet?
It's a web app today, hosted online for this demo. Everything it needs — the AI models, the document templates — runs on the machine itself once loaded; only the initial page load needs a connection.
Did you build all of this yourselves?
Yes, the logic, the checks, and the app are ours. We use well-known, freely available building blocks for the pieces that don't need reinventing — a face-detection model, an OCR engine, a cryptography library — the same way nobody hand-writes their own version of well-tested public tools. What's ours is how they're combined and governed.

Tougher questions hard

The kind of question a more technical or skeptical judge asks to test if you actually understand your own system. Plain-English answer first, technical detail after.

What happens when your AI is wrong?
Nothing dangerous happens — that's the actual design goal. The AI-based checks (forgery pattern, face match) are capped: however confident they are, they can only push a document up to "needs review," never to "reject." Technical: this is enforced in code (core/risk.py), not just policy — a failing AI signal alone cannot cross into the CRITICAL band.
What's your actual accuracy percentage?
We deliberately don't lead with one. A self-reported accuracy number on data we generated ourselves would be close to meaningless — and published research shows even the best real-world systems get roughly one in four unseen forged documents wrong. A confident-sounding accuracy number is usually a red flag, not a strength. Technical: the best team in a 2026 international forgery-detection competition scored 26.5% EER (error rate) on unseen documents — cited in our research notes.
Isn't "Error Level Analysis" (one of your forgery checks) a discredited technique?
You're right, and we say so ourselves in our own code comments — it's unreliable on its own. We include it anyway, but it never affects the score or the decision by itself; it's shown for completeness, clearly labelled as weak.
Your digital signature isn't the real government passport system — so what does it actually prove?
It proves the exact same mathematical property real passport chips use — that nothing has changed since a trusted authority first signed off on it — we just used our own "trusted authority" for the demo instead of the government's official one. Swap in the real one, and none of our code changes, only whose signature we trust.
Can someone tamper with your audit logbook?
Editing any past entry is instantly detectable — every entry is cryptographically linked to the one before it, so changing one breaks the chain and we can point to exactly where. Being fully honest: if someone deletes the most recent few entries entirely (rather than editing one), that specific attack isn't caught yet — we know this, and it's a clearly written next step, not something we're hiding.
Why not just use one big AI model for everything?
Because a single model gives you a single number and no way to challenge it. Ours breaks the decision into independent, explainable pieces — you can see exactly which check failed and why, and a mistake in one piece can't silently overrule a mathematically certain result from another.
Have you actually tested this, or is it just a demo?
108 automated checks (tests) run automatically before we accept any change to the code — covering the ID-check logic, the cryptography, the tamper-proof log, and the forgery detectors. That's not a slide claim, it's a command anyone can run.

What we're upfront and honest about

Say these before you're asked — it reads as confidence, not weakness.

Our demo passport is made up. It's a fictional country's specimen document we generated ourselves, clearly watermarked "not a travel document" — no real government ID is used anywhere.
Our digital signature authority is our own, for the demo — not plugged into the real government passport registry. The cryptography itself is fully real.
Our tamper-proof logbook isn't a blockchain, and doesn't yet catch someone deleting recent entries outright — only editing old ones.
Our fake-document generator only mimics one country's layout. The general-purpose mode (any real document) is separate, and honest when a check doesn't apply.

Glossary — plain English for every term you'll hear

OCR
Software that reads printed text out of an image.
MRZ
The two lines of odd-looking capital letters and numbers at the bottom of a passport photo page — a machine-readable version of your details.
Check digit / checksum
A number that's mathematically derived from the other numbers around it — like a built-in "does this add up?" test.
Cryptographic signature
A digital, unforgeable "wax seal" — mathematical proof that a specific person or system approved a specific piece of data, and that it hasn't changed since.
Hash
A short fingerprint calculated from a file or piece of data — even one changed pixel produces a completely different fingerprint.
Hash chain
A logbook where each new entry includes the fingerprint of the entry before it — edit an old entry, and every entry after it stops matching.
Forensic detection
Looking for physical clues of tampering in the image itself — blur mismatches, pasted-photo edges, screen-glare patterns.
Biometric match
Comparing two faces mathematically and producing a similarity score — not a human eyeballing two photos.
Quality gate
A check that runs before the face match, to make sure the camera photo is even good enough to judge fairly.
Risk band
One of four buckets a document's final score falls into — Low, Medium, High, or Critical.
False positive / false negative
A genuine document wrongly flagged as fake (false positive), or a fake document wrongly passed as genuine (false negative).
Model
A trained piece of software that's learned to recognise a pattern — a face, a piece of text, a forged edge — from lots of examples.
This document covers what's actually built and tested. A few bigger future ideas (an offline mobile version, checks for specific Indian/Nepali scripts, matching one person across multiple past visits) are on our roadmap but not built — if asked, say exactly that: "that's on our roadmap, not in the current build."
